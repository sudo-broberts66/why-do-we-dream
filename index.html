23:14 Awake

Why do we dream what we dream?

Tonight you will lie down, go blind, lose the use of your body and hallucinate for about two hours. By breakfast most of it will be gone. This is one night, followed from the inside.

Before you go under, leave three things from your day. You will meet them again.

Headphones help. What you type stays on this page.

In a few minutes you will lose consciousness.

You do this every night, and it has never once struck you as strange.

The dark behind your eyelids has a name. Eigengrau, “own grey”: the colour your visual system shows you when it has nothing to look at.

From here on, everything you see is made by you.

23:25 · Stage N1 · sleep onset

The gate closes

Deep in the middle of your brain, the thalamus stops passing your senses along. The room is still there. You have stopped receiving it.

The visual cortex does not go quiet. With nothing coming in, it starts to draw.

Lattices. Cobwebs. Tunnels. Spirals.

Everyone sees the same four families of shape, in every culture that has ever been asked. They are the wiring of the visual cortex, seen from the inside.

Heinrich Klüver named them form constants in the 1920s. The mathematics came fifty years later: straight stripes of activity on the cortex look like spirals and tunnels in the eye.

Then the day leaks in. In one experiment, people played a falling-block game for hours. That night, as they drifted off, they saw the blocks falling. So did patients with amnesia, who had no memory of ever having played.

First clueThe dream takes its pictures from your recent life. It does not need your permission, or even your memory.

And then you fall.

23:55 · Stage N3 · deep sleep

The day, replayed

Half an hour in, you reach the bottom. Billions of neurons fire together and fall silent together, about once a second.

Each wave rolls from the front of your head to the back.

Inside every wave, the hippocampus plays back the day. The same neurons fire in the same order, up to twenty times faster than you lived it.

Each burst is sent up to the cortex, which slowly takes the memory over.

In rats you can read which maze the animal ran from the replay alone.

Not everything gets replayed. While you were awake, some moments were tagged: the ones that scared you, surprised you, or mattered. Those go first.

Second clueWhat you dream about is chosen by what you felt.

There are dreams down here, but they are short and plain. A thought. A room.

The long strange ones need a different kind of sleep.

00:52 · REM sleep · first period

Ignition

Ninety minutes in, the slow waves stop.

At the base of the brain, a knot of cells in the pons begins to fire in bursts. Each burst travels up through the thalamus to the back of the head, where seeing happens.

The visual areas light up. So does the amygdala, the brain’s centre for alarm and emotion, which now runs up to 30% hotter than when you are awake.

Beside it, the hippocampus opens the archive.

And the front goes dark. The prefrontal cortex checks logic, keeps track of time, and tells you that can’t be right. In REM it is switched off.

This is why nothing in a dream seems strange.

Then the chemistry changes. Noradrenaline, the brain’s stress signal, falls to almost nothing. It is the only time in the twenty-four hours that it does.

02:25 · REM sleep · second period

And now you are somewhere

Your brain never waits for the world and then looks at it. It guesses what is out there, and uses your senses to correct the guess.

Tonight nothing is correcting it. A dream is what seeing is like with the world taken away.

The pieces are yours.

This is the house you grew up in. An old friend is here. So is a message you never answered, though it looks nothing like itself.

About one dream in fifty replays an event as it happened. Two in three contain pieces of your waking life. The sleeping brain rebuilds; it almost never replays.

Awake, your mind takes the obvious next step. In REM it prefers the distant one. It holds today up against something from fifteen years ago and checks whether they fit.

That is why the staircase becomes a spine becomes a bridge. You are watching a search.

People who were born blind dream in sound, touch and smell.

Third clueWhatever a life is built from, its dreams are built from too.

The horizon is folding over your head. It seems perfectly reasonable.

03:57 · REM sleep · third period

Then it turns

Most of the feeling in dreams is bad. Fear and worry outnumber joy in every large collection of dream reports ever gathered.

The single most common dream on Earth is being chased.

What follows you is usually something of your own. A message you never answered, grown enormous, somewhere behind you.

After being chased come falling, arriving late, sitting an exam you never studied for, and teeth coming loose. The list is nearly the same in every country.

One explanation is that this is practice. For most of human history the things chasing us were real, and a brain that rehearsed its escape at night did better by day.

Fourth clueYou dream your fears because a dream is a safe place to meet them.

And then the ground is not there.

You never land.

04:35 · Stage N2 · between dreams

Not only us

Rats run the day’s maze again in their sleep, neuron for neuron. Zebra finches rehearse their songs without making a sound.

And a sleeping octopus does this.

About once an hour its skin runs through the patterns it wears when it is awake: hunting, hiding, warning. Its brain, for that minute, looks awake too.

The last ancestor we share with an octopus lived more than 500 million years ago and had almost no brain at all.

So either dreaming is very old, or evolution built it twice. Nothing this costly is kept by accident.

05:12 · REM sleep · fourth period

So what is it for?

Nobody knows for certain. These are the seven best ideas.

Memory consolidation

To keep

Sleep is when the day gets filed. Replay moves new memories out of the hippocampus and weaves them into everything you already know. Dreaming may be what that feels like from inside.

People who dreamed about a maze they had just learned got ten times better at it than people who did not.

Emotional processing

To heal

REM replays the hard memories with the stress chemistry switched off. Each night takes a little more of the edge away. You keep what happened and lose some of how much it hurt.

Among people going through a divorce, the ones who dreamed about it were the ones who had recovered a year later.

Threat simulation

To rehearse

A dream is a simulator in which being chased costs nothing. You practise fear at night so that you are faster with it in the day.

The overfitted brain

To generalise

Your days repeat. A brain that learned only from them would memorise its life instead of understanding it. So every night it feeds itself warped, improbable versions.

On this view the strangeness is the purpose.

Network exploration

To explore

The sleeping brain takes a new memory and goes looking for its distant relatives. What else is like this? Most of the links it finds are useless. A few are the idea you wake up with.

Defensive activation

To defend

Brain territory that falls idle starts being taken over by its neighbours within hours. Vision is the one sense the night switches off. Dreams may be how the visual cortex holds its ground until morning.

Activation-synthesis

Or for no reason

The brainstem fires at random. The cortex, which cannot stop making sense of things, makes a story out of the noise.

Even if this is right, the story is still assembled from you.

Nobody has managed to rule any of them out.

A hand is for holding, pointing and waving. Dreaming may be for all seven.

06:12 · REM sleep · fifth and last period

Morning

The last dream of the night is the longest and the strangest. It is the one you might remember.

Might.

The same chemistry that let you dream without fear also left the recorder switched off. Noradrenaline comes back within moments of waking. By then most of the dream has already gone.

Light, through your eyelids.

So, once more. Why do we dream what we dream?

Because of what happened to you.

Because of what you felt about it.

Because of everything your life has been built from.

Because of what you are afraid of, and what you are still waiting for.

Every night your brain takes the newest pieces of your life, picks out the ones with the most feeling on them, and holds each one up against everything you have ever known, looking for where it fits.

It does this with logic switched off and fear turned down, so that it can try combinations you would never allow yourself awake. You experience the search as a story.

It is built from a set of memories that nobody else has. It will never be built the same way twice.

You will spend about six years of your life in there. You will remember almost none of it.

It was still you.
